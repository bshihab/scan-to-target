From MRI Scanner to Electrode Target

A pipeline that starts with the files an MRI scanner writes and ends with an electrode target in a robot's coordinates. It finds the hand area of motor cortex from a finger-tapping fMRI scan, lines that map up with the anatomy, and lets you plan the insertion path in a browser:

  • My DICOM reader matches dcm2niix voxel for voxel on 29 real GE and Siemens series, including tilted scans, Siemens mosaics and compressed files, and it refuses a CT with an 18.5 degree gantry tilt instead of loading it wrong
  • On a simulated scan with known misalignment and EPI-style distortion, the fMRI-to-anatomy registration lands a median 0.33 mm from the truth
  • On the real scans it gives the same answer from very different starting points and agrees with itself across two scanning days to 0.91 mm, but it disagrees with SimpleITK by about 10 mm, a pass bar I set and missed
  • The browser planner draws three slices plus views along and across the insertion path, and a left-right phantom test checks it in both radiological and neurological display conventions
  • For the robot step, my predicted target error matches 20,000 simulated registrations within 0.6%; four fiducials bunched on the forehead put the target 11 times further off than six spread around the skull
  • [M7: across 10 volunteers scanned on two days, the planned target moved a median of X mm with the peak rule and Y mm with the cluster rule]

How it works

Every image is an array plus one 4x4 matrix that maps voxel indices to millimetres in the scanner's frame. DICOM stores that geometry in its own patient frame (x to the patient's left, y to the back), NIfTI in the opposite sign for x and y, and most bugs in this kind of software come from mixing them. Every point in a plan carries the name of its frame.

The fMRI goes through motion correction, then a general linear model that compares finger blocks against the foot and lips blocks. The fMRI is registered to the anatomical scan in two stages: mutual information gets it close, then boundary-based registration (the method behind FreeSurfer's bbregister) finds the white-matter surface in the anatomy and turns the fMRI until the grey/white contrast lines up along the whole surface. A template brain from MNI gives a brain mask and approximate MNI coordinates for each point.

The planner reslices the anatomy on the fly and draws the fMRI map by carrying each pixel's position through the registration into the map's own grid, so nothing is resampled ahead of time. You drop a target and an entry point; it warns when the entry is off the scalp and can slide it onto the scalp along the planned line. The exported plan goes through a fiducial registration into a simulated robot frame, with the target error predicted by Fitzpatrick's formula.

The hard part

I wrote pass bars before each run and kept the failures. Two registration runs failed before the current method. The first disagreed with SimpleITK by 14 to 19 mm; overlays showed SimpleITK pulled onto the scalp, but my own answer also moved when I started it from different places, by up to 2.4 mm. A crude skull strip didn't fix it. Boundary-based registration with a template brain mask did: the same answer from every start, and a loop through both scanning days that closes to under a millimetre.

Then I had an independent agent review the code cold. It found 11 problems. The worst was a coordinate bug: exported plans gave the target's voxel index in the cropped volume the planner draws, while naming the original scan file, about 3 cm off. My checks hadn't caught it because they compared a formula with itself. The replacement checks recompute every matrix from the source files and render a left-right phantom through the planner's real drawing code; swapping left and right now fails six of them.

One mistake was my own reasoning. The first finger map lit up most of the brain in one session, and I blamed head and jaw movement during the foot and lips blocks. The real cause was the first volume of each run, 26% brighter because the scanner hadn't settled, which was also my motion-correction reference. I withdrew the first explanation in the log.

What it doesn't do yet

The fMRI isn't corrected for EPI distortion, because this dataset has no field map for the run, so near the sinuses the map can sit a few millimetres off. Registration is rigid and nothing accounts for the brain shifting once the skull is open. The robot and tracker are simulated. Left and right come from the scan headers: my code's handling is tested, but nothing in this dataset can confirm the headers themselves. The Google Cloud DICOMweb step is written and tested against a local stand-in server, not yet against Google's API.

I built this with Claude Code (Claude Opus 5.5), which wrote the code and the first draft of this write-up. The data sources, code, every number and every failed run are in the repository.
